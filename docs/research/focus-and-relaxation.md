# Focus and relaxation: evidence review for Juglar

Reviewed 22 October 2026. Scope: passive screen visuals accompanying user-selected music, especially while doing another task. This is a targeted web literature review, not a registered systematic review. Searches covered restoration, simulated nature/fire, ambient displays, fractals, audiovisual music, motion/color, sensory entrainment, and photosensitivity standards. Peer-reviewed syntheses and experiments take priority; standards supply safety requirements. Sample sizes below distinguish recruited from analyzed participants where available. Failure to find a replication is not proof that none exists.

Ratings describe support for the **specific finding**, not the reputation of its source: **Strong** = convergent, substantial evidence or authoritative safety requirements; **Moderate** = convergent but limited/context-dependent evidence; **Weak** = small, mixed, biased, or indirect evidence; **Speculative** = untested transfer to Juglar or a proposed engineering choice. These are editorial ratings, not formal GRADE assessments. Physiological arousal, feeling relaxed, attention-test performance, and clinical improvement are different outcomes.

## 1. TL;DR

- **Moderate:** nature exposure can modestly improve some cognitive outcomes. A 2025 synthesis found a small overall benefit, but no reliable pooled benefit specifically for vigilance/sustained attention. It does not establish that an animated desktop background improves work performance. [3][r3] [4][r4]
- **Weak:** simulated nature often improves reported mood/stress, but physiological findings and study quality are less convincing. Abstract generative imagery is a further extrapolation. [5][r5] [6][r6] [9][r9]
- **Moderate for preference; Weak for stress:** statistical fractals around **D = 1.3–1.5** are reasonable aesthetic candidates. They are not a validated therapeutic dose; exact repeating fractals can elicit different preferences. [10][r10] [11][r11] [12][r12] [13][r13]
- **Strong as a music-intervention baseline; Speculative for added synchronization:** music interventions can reduce stress on average. No controlled evidence located establishes an additional focus/relaxation benefit from Juglar-like beat-synchronized visuals over the same music alone. [15][r15] [17][r17]
- **Moderate for immediate affect; Speculative for exact settings:** motion, saturation, and brightness can increase arousal. Slow, subdued visuals are a defensible starting hypothesis, with static/off options; there is no established calming speed or universal calming color temperature. [9][r9] [18][r18] [19][r19] [20][r20]
- **Weak for therapeutic benefit:** 40 Hz stimulation can evoke frequency-locked EEG responses, but this does not establish better cognition or relaxation. Alzheimer's research is medical territory and does not justify an entrainment mode. [23][r23] [24][r24] [25][r25] [26][r26]
- **Strong for risk reduction, not a safety guarantee:** enforce flash and pattern constraints in every mode, prohibit saturated-red flashing, and provide immediate pause/static controls and reduced-motion support. [27][r27] [28][r28] [29][r29] [31][r31] [32][r32]

## 2. Ambient visuals, attention, and stress

**ART mechanism — Speculative as an explanation for Juglar.** Kaplan and Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory proposes that effortless engagement ("soft fascination"), together with being away, extent, and compatibility, permits recovery from directed-attention fatigue. It is a theory of restorative experience, not a prescription to add stimulation during demanding work. An abstract animation is not automatically softly fascinating merely because it is slow. [1][r1] [2][r2]

**Attention restoration — Moderate for nature; Weak for sustained attention; Speculative for concurrent abstract backgrounds.** Ohly et al. reviewed **31 studies**: three pooled attention measures favored nature; ten others did not show marked benefits. Small samples and heterogeneous methods limited inference. The newer 2025 review included **80 studies/273 outcomes**, finding an overall **g = 0.17, 95% CI 0.11–0.23**. Working memory and attentional control showed reliable benefits; vigilance/sustained attention, across **23 studies**, did not. Prediction intervals were broad. These overlapping syntheses indicate partial convergence, not uniform replication. A duration association peaking around 30 minutes is observational across experiments, not a prescribed session length. Recovery after exposure does not establish improved performance during continuous visual exposure. [3][r3] [4][r4]

**Nature on screens — Weak overall, with a more consistent positive-affect signal.** Li et al. included **28 studies from 26 publications**, with study samples **20–269, median 69**. Positive affect improved (SMD **0.40**, 95% CI 0.22–0.58; **11 studies, n = 849**); perceived stress decreased (**−0.38**, CI −0.71 to −0.06; only **2 studies, n = 146**). Pooled physiological outcomes were not convincingly improved; all studies had moderate/high risk of bias. A 2025 review of **24 studies** also found benefits on symptom scales, but rated all outcomes **very low certainty**: its stress analysis involved **6 studies, n = 250**, with **I² = 81%**. Thus apparent replication in ratings has important bias and heterogeneity qualifications. Neither synthesis isolates abstract visuals with arbitrary music. [5][r5] [6][r6]

**Screen fire — Weak.** Lynn's **three randomized crossover studies, n = 226 combined**, found blood-pressure decreases particularly for fire **with its crackling sound** and longer exposure. This supports a short-term multisensory effect under those conditions, not silent flames, generated particles, arbitrary songs, lasting stress relief, or treatment of hypertension. No independent direct replication was established in this search. [7][r7]

**Ambient-display labs — Weak and mixed.** DeLight tested ambient lighting with **12 participants** during arithmetic and **20** during relaxation training; physiological analyses lost some recordings. Its promising results combined lighting with HRV feedback and deliberate breathing, so passive visuals cannot take the credit. More directly relevant, a 2026 experiment (**n = 67**) compared static, naturally moving, and artificially moving abstract light patterns after a stressor. Natural motion exceeded artificial motion on HRV only after outlier removal; neither exceeded static on that measure, and other stress outcomes were null. Both moving versions were rated more distracting/stressful than static. These are distinct protocols, not replicated demonstrations that ambient animation improves focus. [8][r8] [9][r9]

## 3. Fractals and visual complexity

**Preference — Moderate, stimulus-dependent.** Taylor's University of Oregon research program identifies low-to-middle complexity, often **D = 1.3–1.5**, as preferred for statistical fractals. Robles et al. analyzed **82 adults and 96 children**, finding a statistical-fractal preference peak near **D = 1.3**, but preference for exact fractals rose toward higher D. Bies et al.'s two experiments likewise found higher-D preferences for exact repeating patterns, with symmetry/recursion interactions. This is replication of a conditional aesthetic pattern, not a universal optimum. Individual differences matter. [10][r10] [11][r11] [12][r12]

**Physiological stress — Weak.** Taylor's 2006 report describes a preliminary fractal reanalysis of a **24-person** skin-conductance experiment using different wall images during mental work. Image content and D were not independently manipulated across many matched stimuli. Hägerhäll et al. recruited **32, analyzed 31**: silhouettes near **D = 1.3** produced greater frontal alpha **and** parietal beta. That mixed EEG response is not proof of calm or improved attention. [10][r10] [13][r13]

There is some later independent physiological work: Ibrahim et al. (2025) compared fractal wall finishes in VR and reported mid-D cardiac recovery benefits. However, the abstract also reports the smallest skin-conductance increase in the **low-D** condition. The accessible publisher material did not establish a verifiable sample size for this review; this study is therefore supporting context, not a basis for a precise effect estimate. No large, preregistered, independently replicated estimate of a D-specific stress benefit was established here. Do not repeat a headline such as "fractals reduce stress by 60%" as an expected user benefit. [14][r14] [10][r10]

**Transfer to shaders — Speculative.** D describes a measured structure and scale range, not a generic "complexity" knob. The line/contour dimensions in these studies cannot be assigned directly to a filled image or 3D noise surface. Juglar should define an extracted contour/edge representation, estimator, and scale interval before reporting D; octaves or noise roughness alone do not validate it. Treat **1.3–1.5** as a testable aesthetic target for comparable statistical contours, not a physiological specification. [10][r10] [11][r11] [12][r12]

## 4. Music plus visuals

**Music baseline — Strong for an average stress effect in studied interventions, with limited transfer.** De Witte et al.'s **104 RCTs, n = 9,617** found effects of **d = 0.380** on physiological and **0.545** on psychological stress outcomes. These were heterogeneous listening/therapy interventions and settings, not evidence that any song relaxes any listener. Music therapy is not equivalent to running a visualizer. [15][r15]

**Focus baseline — Moderate that effects depend on task/music/listener.** Cheah et al. reviewed **95 articles/154 experiments, n = 6,246**. Many effects were null; detrimental findings predominated where effects occurred, particularly with lyrics for verbal memory/reading and with difficult tasks. Its synthesis used direction-based vote counting rather than pooled effect sizes. It does not support "music improves focus" as a general premise to which visuals can simply add a benefit. [16][r16]

**Added visuals/synchronization — Weak for audiovisual mood effects; Speculative for an incremental focus or relaxation benefit.** Wang et al.'s peer-reviewed Chinese CHI study (**100 participants, five groups**) compared four emotionally congruent music/digital-art videos with control; happy/sad combinations reduced reported state anxiety more effectively. It did **not** isolate music alone, visuals alone, or beat synchrony. Emotional congruence and temporal synchronization are different manipulations. [17][r17]

No controlled study located in this review establishes that slow, procedural, music-synchronized visuals outperform the **same music alone** for sustained attention, anxiety, or relaxation. Nor was a replicated optimal audiovisual delay or modulation depth found. Benefits, no effect, and added distraction all remain possible; the static-versus-motion result above makes distraction a concrete concern. Smoothing the music response is an engineering hypothesis, not an evidence-based enhancement to music therapy. [9][r9] [17][r17]

## 5. Motion, speed, color, and contrast

**Motion/arousal — Moderate for immediate laboratory responses; Weak for speed-to-relaxation rules.** Simons et al. reproduced motion-related increases in arousal with a between-subjects design after earlier within-subject experiments. This is evidence about attention to the image, not better attention to another task. A speed experiment in animated ads (**n = 47**) shows speed matters, but advertising stimuli do not determine an optimum for abstract visuals. Together with the mixed ambient-light experiment, this supports testing slow/static versions rather than assuming motion is restorative. No validated calming range in pixels/second or degrees/second was located. [18][r18] [19][r19] [9][r9]

**Saturation/brightness — Moderate; hue/temperature prescriptions — Weak.** Wilms and Oberfeld (**n = 62**, 30-second exposures) independently varied hue, saturation, and brightness. Brighter and more saturated colors increased reported arousal and skin-conductance responses; red was more arousing than blue/green in that experiment. Bright/saturated colors also increased pleasantness, illustrating that pleasant and low-arousal are different. Color research has substantial context and generalization limitations. No universal calming palette, contrast ratio, or Kelvin setting for a visualizer follows from it. [20][r20] [21][r21]

**Evening light is a separate issue — Moderate for prolonged bright-screen effects; Speculative for Juglar palettes.** Chang et al.'s crossover experiment (**n = 12**, four hours/night for five nights) found circadian/sleep effects from a light-emitting reader versus print. This is not a test of a dim blue shader versus a dim amber shader. A visually "cool" palette and a light source's correlated color temperature are different quantities; RGB values alone do not specify retinal exposure. Offer dim/warmer choices, but make no sleep or melatonin claim. [22][r22] [21][r21]

## 6. Audiovisual entrainment and flicker

**Evoked EEG responses — Moderate; improved focus/relaxation — Weak.** A 2025 structured narrative review found heterogeneous, sometimes null AVE findings and inadequate standardization. Wu et al. recruited **22, analyzed 20** healthy adults in a randomized-order crossover: **30 minutes** of 40 Hz light/sound evoked online EEG responses, without significant subsequent attention/perception/memory benefits or persistent entrainment. One participant withdrew because of light intolerance. A frequency peak during stimulation is not equivalent to a durable, beneficial change in endogenous brain activity. [23][r23] [24][r24]

**Tsai lab/GENUS — promising medical research, Weak for human therapeutic efficacy.** The original mouse experiments reported reduced amyloid with 40 Hz light and later combined light/sound; an independent mouse investigation did not reproduce reliable amyloid/microglial benefits. Animal evidence is therefore not uniformly replicated. [33][r33] [34][r34] [35][r35]

Chan et al.'s human feasibility cohorts included **25 cognitively normal volunteers, 16 with mild Alzheimer's dementia, and 2 monitored epilepsy patients**; their randomized pilot involved **15 Alzheimer's patients**, with daily one-hour stimulation and exploratory three-month outcomes. These small, selected, supervised samples do not establish consumer safety or efficacy. [25][r25]

The separate **76-person OVERTURE** randomized, sham-controlled six-month trial did not show a significant primary cognitive-functional outcome, despite some positive secondary outcomes. A 2025 meta-analysis (**11 studies, n = 341**) found **no significant cognitive benefit** (SMD **0.16**, CI **−0.36 to 0.68**) or daily-function benefit; heterogeneity was high. Biomarker/structural signals do not substitute for clinical benefit. [26][r26] [36][r36]

**Juglar implication — Speculative benefit, clear safety conflict.** Ordinary music-reactive motion is not a tested GENUS protocol. Do not implement alpha/theta/gamma flicker presets or claim brainwave optimization, Alzheimer's prevention, amyloid clearance, ADHD treatment, or anxiety treatment. A visible 40 Hz strobe also conflicts with the all-mode flash policy below; "imperceptible" flicker is not a waiver. [23][r23] [24][r24] [27][r27] [29][r29]

## 7. Safety evidence and accessibility

**Photosensitivity — Strong basis for risk-reduction requirements.** WCAG 2.3.1 permits no more than three flashes in any one second **or** flashing below its general/red thresholds. ITU-R **BT.1702-3 (2023)** supplies broadcast luminance, red, and pattern guidance. Their spatial models differ: WCAG uses a visual-angle region; ITU uses displayed-screen area. Neither makes all viewers safe, and warnings or pause controls cannot replace preventing hazardous output. Adopt their limits as engineering requirements for this native app without claiming WCAG conformance from this review alone. [27][r27] [28][r28] [29][r29]

**Motion sensitivity — Moderate; no universal safe speed.** A systematic review of **54 studies** identifies visual-motion hypersensitivity across vestibular disorders, migraine, concussion, and other groups. W3C explains that motion can provoke vestibular symptoms and provides reduced-motion mechanisms. The CSS preference is relevant to a web interface; Rust/native rendering must also read the platform preference or offer an equivalent persistent setting. A lower speed alone is insufficient for someone needing a static display. [30][r30] [31][r31] [32][r32]

## 8. Design implications for Juglar

**All exact settings in this table are Speculative prototype defaults, not experimentally established thresholds or promises of benefit.** They translate the directional findings above into measurable starting points. Focus means minimizing competition with a separate task; relaxation means optional low-arousal viewing. Users should be able to select static/off immediately. [4][r4] [9][r9] [20][r20] [30][r30]

Use `S = min(viewport_width, viewport_height)`. Speeds are **visible feature displacement**, not shader-time multipliers: 0.005 S/s means 5.4 px/s for S = 1080. Geometry and viewing distance change angular velocity, so these values are not vestibular safety limits. Define luminance `Y` in linear sRGB, normalized 0–1; the display's actual cd/m² cannot be inferred from those values. [28][r28] [29][r29] [30][r30]

| Parameter | Focus mode | Relaxation mode | Rationale and limits of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Motion | Default 0.002 S/s; adjustable **0–0.005 S/s**. Fixed camera. | Default 0.003 S/s; adjustable **0–0.010 S/s**. Fixed camera. | Reduce competing motion; static must remain available. Exact ranges are untested. [9][r9] [18][r18] |
| Complexity | Optional statistical contours at measured **D = 1.3–1.5**; default target 1.4. | Same candidate range; allow user preference rather than claiming an optimum. | Evidence is stronger for liking than stress. Do not apply this D to arbitrary noise/3D surfaces. [10][r10] [11][r11] [12][r12] |
| Palette | Muted gray-blue/green or neutral; HSV saturation **0.10–0.30**. | Muted neutral, blue-green, or optional amber; saturation **0.10–0.35**. | Lower saturation is a plausible arousal choice; HSV numbers and hue choices are product hypotheses. Amber is not a sleep treatment. [20][r20] [21][r21] [22][r22] |
| Visual intensity | Final visualizer pixels **Y ≤ 0.20**; spatial peak-to-trough **ΔY ≤ 0.10**. | **Y ≤ 0.25**; spatial **ΔY ≤ 0.15**. | Caps apply after bloom/tone mapping, with an independent dimmer down to zero. These are SDR appearance caps, not safe physical luminances. Text/controls need separate readable styling. [20][r20] [28][r28] [29][r29] |
| Transitions | Palette/scene crossfades **10–20 s**; hold a scene **≥120 s**. | Crossfades **10–30 s**; hold **≥60 s**. | Avoid frequent novelty and abrupt changes; exact durations have no validated relaxation effect. [9][r9] [18][r18] |
| Music response | Smooth an energy envelope with **2 s attack / 5 s release**; global luminance modulation **0**; geometry displacement modulation **≤5%** of its configured scale. | **1 s attack / 4 s release**; luminance modulation **≤0.02 Y peak-to-peak**; displacement modulation **≤10%**. | Preserve a gentle connection without per-beat flashes. Time constants/depths are hypotheses, and smoothing alone does not ensure safety. [9][r9] [17][r17] [27][r27] |
| Camera/global flow | **0** camera rotation, zoom, shake, or tunnel/approach flow. | Same starting restriction. | Motion-sensitive users need control; no demonstrated universal safe flow speed. [30][r30] [31][r31] |
| Reduced motion | **0** continuous motion and audio modulation; static frame or blank output. | Same. | Honor OS preference before the first frame and during changes; provide a persistent override. [31][r31] [32][r32] |

Keep output bounds independent of input music: loudness, tempo changes, clipping, silence, and device reconnection must never increase these caps. This is a proposed safeguard, not an efficacy finding. Before claiming performance benefits, compare **music alone, static visual + music, unsynchronized moving visual + music, and synchronized visual + identical music**, with counterbalanced order, a prespecified sustained-attention measure, subjective distraction/relaxation, and discomfort/dropouts. Such a test would address the comparator and outcome gaps above. [4][r4] [9][r9] [17][r17] [24][r24]

## 9. Hard safety limits

These are proposed requirements for **every** mode, preset, shader, transition, startup/recovery screen, and user setting. Standards-based boundaries are distinguished from stricter Juglar policies. They are not implemented by this document.

| Item | Enforceable rule | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flash count | **Juglar policy:** no intentional strobing; allow at most **2 qualifying general flashes in every rolling 1.000 s**, irrespective of area. Count opposing transitions, not frames. WCAG's outer boundary is **≤3**, not an average of 3/s. | Stricter margin under [27][r27] [28][r28]. |
| General-flash detection | Flag opposing luminance changes with **ΔY ≥0.10** when darker **Y <0.80**. Detect extrema across time, not merely adjacent-frame differences. A slow ramp can accumulate a qualifying change. | WCAG definition. [28][r28] |
| Flash area | WCAG's area exception is **≤0.006 sr within any 10° visual-field region** (about 25% of that region). **Juglar policy:** permit **0 pixels** to exceed the rate rule; do not use a small-area exception. | Viewing distance is unknown. The illustrative **341×256** region at 1024×768 is the inspection region; its quarter is **21,824 px**, not 25% of the entire screen. [27][r27] [28][r28] |
| Saturated red | **0 qualifying red flashes.** Current W3C guidance flags opposing transitions involving linear **R/(R+G+B) ≥0.8** and CIE 1976 UCS chromaticity separation **>0.2**. Treat black separately. As a conservative palette guard, keep animated nonblack colors at **R/(R+G+B) ≤0.75**, and still check final composited output. | This margin is Juglar policy. It supplements, rather than replaces, temporal detection. [27][r27] |
| HDR/physical luminance | Keep initial delivery **SDR**. If HDR is introduced, test actual output: for darker luminance **<160 cd/m²**, a **≥20 cd/m²** change is potentially harmful; at **≥160 cd/m²**, use a conservative **Michelson contrast ≥1/17** detector. | ITU's HDR criterion says **>1/17**; using ≥ adds margin. No SDR pixel cap alone guarantees physical safety. [29][r29] |
| Repetitive patterns | **Juglar policy:** no more than **5 discernible light/dark stripe pairs** in a regular pattern, including radial stripes/repeated rows. | ITU's informative guidance flags >5 qualifying-contrast pairs covering **>40%** when static or **>25%** when oscillating/reversing. Juglar should not rely on those area exemptions. [29][r29] |
| Reduced motion/pause | Reduced-motion output: **0** animation, camera motion, and audio-driven modulation. Provide a direct keyboard/button pause and blank option. Do not wait **5 s** before making controls available. | WCAG 2.2.2's >5 s condition applies to automatic motion alongside other content; this immediate-control rule is stricter product policy. [31][r31] [32][r32] [37][r37] |

Implementation notes: linearize sRGB channels before calculating `Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B` (decode each normalized channel `c` as `c/12.92` for `c ≤0.04045`, otherwise `((c+0.055)/1.055)^2.4`). Test rendered output at its largest supported display size and aggregate simultaneous affected regions across layers; whole-frame averages can conceal local flashes. [28][r28] [27][r27]

Proposed enforcement: analyze a candidate frame **before presentation**, and suppress/freeze it if it would violate a limit. Track timestamps across frame-rate changes, not fixed frame counts. Keep the safety pass after bloom, exposure, palette conversion, and compositing; finite capture tests alone cannot guarantee all procedural seeds or audio inputs. Validate adversarial audio, seeds, cuts, and frame drops, then analyze captures using an independent flash/pattern analyzer. **HardingFPA** is one commercial implementation of international flash/pattern guidance, not a separate numerical standard or a certificate Juglar already holds. [29][r29] [38][r38]

No flash threshold guarantees absence of seizures, migraine, or vestibular discomfort in every viewer. Do not describe a passing visual as "epilepsy-safe." [29][r29] [30][r30]

## 10. What we must not claim

Suitable README wording, once the settings exist:

> Focus and relaxation presets offer slower motion, subdued palettes, and adjustable music responsiveness. Choose static or reduced-motion output at any time. These presets have not been shown to improve attention or treat stress or anxiety.

Avoid "scientifically proven to improve focus," "reduces cortisol/blood pressure," "reduces stress by 60%," "therapeutic fractals," "brainwave optimization," "40 Hz neuroprotection," and claims to treat ADHD, anxiety, insomnia, or Alzheimer's disease. Current evidence does not establish those benefits for Juglar; selected clinical protocols and nature-viewing experiments are not validation of this product. [4][r4] [5][r5] [10][r10] [15][r15] [23][r23] [26][r26] [36][r36]

Describe parameters and user choice. Reserve causal benefit claims for direct, adequately controlled evidence on the implemented product; even strong evidence about music alone would not establish a therapeutic claim for the added visuals. Do not claim safety certification, full WCAG compliance, or a passed Harding test without the corresponding implementation and evaluation. [15][r15] [17][r17] [27][r27] [38][r38]
