Beats and flashes

Juglar can tell when something happens in the music, a kick, a snare, a drop, and it makes sure the picture never answers with flashing. This page is about both. The first half mirrors crates/dsp/src/onset.rs, the detector of onsets, beats and novelty; the second, crates/render/src/limiter.rs and limiter.wgsl, the flash limiter every frame passes through. The theory is in notes 07 and 09.

01Loud is not the same as new

The obvious way to find a drum hit is to wait for the sound to get loud. Below is two seconds of drums, repeated four times, built from the pattern in the grid: click a square to add or remove a hit. Under them, a pad (a soft chord) swells and fades every four seconds, and some noise hisses throughout. All of it is synthesized on this page; Listen plays it.

Turn the pad up and watch the loudness: the swell lifts it as much as the drums do, and a threshold on loudness would either miss the drums in the quiet parts or fire on the swell in the loud ones. Turn the noise up and the drums sink into it. What marks the start of a sound is not that it is loud, but that energy appears where there was none a moment ago.

02Spectral flux, in three regions

Every 10.7 ms (a hop), Juglar takes the newest 21 ms of sound, transforms it (explorable 01), and measures about 18 bands in decibels: narrow in the bass, wide in the treble. For each band it subtracts the level two hops earlier; rises count, falls count as zero. The rises, averaged over a group of bands, are the spectral flux. It is computed separately for three regions: low (kicks, bass), middle (snares, voices) and high (hi-hats, cymbals).

Three details make it work. Switch each off and see what breaks:

  • Two hops back, not one. Each 21 ms window overlaps the previous one by half, so an attack lands half in one window and half in the next. Comparing with two hops back compares windows that don't overlap, and the whole attack shows up in one rise.
  • Rises measured against each band's noise. A band only a few frequency steps wide is a shaky measurement: in steady noise its level jumps by several decibels from hop to hop (6 dB for a band one step wide, divided by the square root of its width). Dividing each rise by that number counts it in "how unusual for this band". Without it, the narrow bass bands drown everything in noise: turn the noise up and watch the extra onsets appear.
  • Three regions. Averaged over the whole spectrum, a kick drum fills only the lowest bands and gets diluted by everything else.

03When is a rise an onset?

Flux is never zero in real music. An onset is a hop whose flux stands out: it is a local peak (higher than the hop before, no lower than the hop after, so onsets are reported one hop late), it clears an adaptive threshold (the last second's mean plus three times its typical deviation: high in busy music, low in a quiet ballad), it clears a floor set from the statistics of noise (so steady hiss never fires), and the region hasn't fired in the last 100 ms. The levels are measured relative to the loudest band, so the volume doesn't matter.

A beat is an onset in the low or middle region: kicks and snares, what you tap your foot to. Hi-hats are found in the high region but don't make beats. Lower the spread above to 1 and the threshold hugs the flux: every wobble becomes an onset. Raise it to 6 and quiet hits are missed.

Optional, for the curious: the peak picking (crates/dsp/src/onset.rs) 
fn hop(&mut self, flux: f32, refractory_samples: u64) -> bool {
    let n = self.past.len() as f32;
    let mean = self.past.iter().sum::<f32>() / n;
    let deviation = self.past.iter().map(|f| (f - mean).abs()).sum::<f32>() / n;
    self.threshold = (mean + THRESHOLD_SPREAD * deviation).max(self.floor);
    self.past[self.write] = flux;
    self.write = (self.write + 1) % self.past.len();

    self.recent = [self.recent[1], self.recent[2], flux];
    let [before, candidate, after] = self.recent;
    let rested = self.since.is_none_or(|since| since >= refractory_samples);
    candidate > before && candidate >= after && candidate > self.threshold && rested
}

04Novelty: the music changed

A drop, a chorus, a breakdown: the song changes character, and the change lasts. Juglar keeps two running averages of every band's level, one over half a second, one over four. While the music keeps its character they agree; when it changes, the short one moves first. How far apart they are, in decibels across all bands, is the novelty: 2.5 dB is 0, 6 dB is 1.

05What counts as a flash

Now the other side: what the picture does with all this. A small share of people can have a seizure triggered by flashing light, and many more get headaches from it. Juglar's rule (from its research notes, built on WCAG 2.3.1) is at most two flashes in any second, anywhere on the screen. A flash is a pair of opposing changes in luminance of 0.1 or more (black is 0, white 1): two transitions. Four transitions per rolling second is the limit.

Counting them is not comparing frames: a brightness that rises 0.03 per frame for five frames never changes much from one frame to the next, but it swung by 0.15. So the counter follows the luminance, remembers its last extreme, and counts a transition when it swings back from that extreme by 0.1 or more.

Below 2 flashes per second, or with a swing under 0.1, the limiter changes nothing. Above, it lets the first two flashes through and then holds the light where it is until a second has passed since the oldest transition: the strobe becomes two pulses per second.

Optional, for the curious: one cell of the limiter (crates/render/src/limiter.rs) 
pub fn step(&mut self, y: f32, dt: f32) -> (f32, f32) {
    self.ages.iter_mut().for_each(|a| *a += dt);
    let recent = self.ages.iter().filter(|&&a| a < WINDOW).count();
    let (mut low, mut high) = (-1.0, 2.0);
    if recent >= MAX_TRANSITIONS {
        if self.direction > 0.0 { low = self.extreme - (DELTA - MARGIN); }
        else if self.direction < 0.0 { high = self.extreme + (DELTA - MARGIN); }
    }
    let mut alpha: f32 = 1.0;
    if y < low { alpha = (low - self.last) / (y - self.last); }
    else if y > high { alpha = (high - self.last) / (y - self.last); }
    let alpha = alpha.clamp(0.0, 1.0);
    let shown = self.last + (y - self.last) * alpha;
    // ...then follow `shown`: a new extreme, or a transition.

The GPU runs the same steps in limiter.wgsl, for every cell of the screen at once.

06The limiter on a picture

A flash in a corner is a flash even if the rest of the screen is calm, so the limiter watches the screen in small square cells, each with its own count, and holds back only the cells that would break the rule. The held cell shows a mix of what it showed last frame and the new frame: just enough of the new frame to keep the swing under 0.1. It all happens on the GPU in the same frame, before anything reaches the screen.

The trembling bright line is the limiter's first finding in Juglar itself. Tide's ring was a bright line one or two pixels wide drawn from the waveform, which changes every frame: the line trembled by a fraction of a pixel, and the pixels along it blinked several times a second. At a distance it looks like motion; at the scale of a pixel it is flashing, and the limiter held the cells along the ring, leaving specks. Smoothing the waveform over time once (each frame moving part of the way toward the new one) only halved the holds there: a one-stage average still takes a small random step every frame. Three stages in a row leave a smooth path, and nothing was held. Try the three settings. This small picture trembles more gently than Tide's ring did, so here one stage already helps a lot; turn the trembling all the way up and it still leaves cells held, while three leave none.

The fine checkerboard shows what the limiter can't see: squares swapping light and dark keep each cell's average the same, so no cell ever swings. Flicker finer than a cell is invisible to it, and scenes must not draw it. The moving light passes each cell once in a while: two transitions, not a flash.

07No saturated red

Flickering saturated red is a hazard of its own: WCAG counts a color as saturated red when red carries 80% or more of its light. Juglar's palettes keep every color at 75% or less, and as a last guard the limiter moves any color above 75% toward a gray of the same luminance, exactly far enough. Keeping the luminance means the guard can never create a flash itself.
