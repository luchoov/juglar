Start here

Juglar is a program that listens to whatever music your computer is playing and draws moving images that follow it. This page explains, from zero, every idea the rest of the documentation relies on. No music theory and no programming needed. If you have headphones or speakers, you can also listen.

01Sound is air moving

A speaker is a thin surface that pushes the air forward and pulls it back, very fast. Those pushes travel through the air as waves and move your eardrum the same way. That's all sound is: air pressure going up and down.

Two things describe a simple sound:

  • Frequency: how many times per second the air goes back and forth. It is measured in hertz (Hz). Few times per second sounds low (a bass); many times sounds high (a whistle). People hear roughly from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Amplitude: how far the air moves each time. Bigger sounds louder.

Press play and move the sliders. The graph always shows the same 20 milliseconds (two hundredths of a second), so a higher frequency fits more waves into it.

02How a computer stores sound

A computer can't store a smooth curve. Instead, it measures the height of the wave at regular moments and keeps the list of numbers. Each measurement is a sample. Your computer takes 48,000 samples every second, usually twice (left and right speaker).

Each sample is just a number between −1 and 1: 0 is silence, and ±1 is the loudest the speaker can go. So, to a computer, a song is a very long list of numbers. Move the slider: with few samples the dots can't follow the wave; with enough of them, they trace it closely.

03Real music is many sounds at once

A song is never one clean wave. A drum, a bass, a voice and a cymbal all play together, and the speaker can only move one way at a time, so their waves simply add up into one complicated shape. Turn the parts on and off, and listen.

Looking at the bright line, you can't tell what's playing. Juglar's first job is to undo that sum: to find out how much bass, how much voice and how much cymbal there is, many times per second. The tool that does it is the Fourier transform, explained in explorable 01.

04What Juglar does, step by step

  • Listen. Windows mixes the sound of every app before sending it to the speakers. Juglar asks Windows for a copy of that mix, so it works with any music player.
  • Analyze. About 30 times per second, Juglar takes the last 43 milliseconds of sound and measures how much energy there is in the lows, mids and highs.
  • Signals. Those measurements become a few numbers between 0 and 1, smoothed so they move like music: they jump on a drum hit and fade out after it.
  • Generate. The generator turns the signals into a scene: shapes, motion and colors chosen to fit the music.
  • Draw. The graphics card (GPU), the part of the computer built to color millions of pixels at once, draws each frame. The main processor (CPU) stays almost idle, so Juglar can run next to a game.

The goal that makes Juglar different is in the vision: to listen like a musician, recognizing when the chorus comes back, which key the song is in, and when a drop is about to land, not just how loud it is.

05Words you will see

Hertz (Hz)
Times per second. 440 Hz is the note A that orchestras tune to. 1 kHz is 1,000 Hz.
Sample
One measurement of the sound wave. 48,000 of them make one second.
Decibel (dB)
A way to measure loudness that matches how ears work: every −6 dB halves the wave's size. Juglar uses dBFS, where 0 is the loudest possible and quieter sounds are negative.
Bass, mid, treble
Low, middle and high frequencies. Roughly: kick drum and bass guitar; voices and guitars; cymbals and hi-hats.
Spectrum
A picture of how much of each frequency is present right now, from low to high.
Fourier transform (FFT)
The math that turns a list of samples into a spectrum. FFT is the fast way to compute it.
Band
A range of frequencies added together into one number, like "all the bass".
Envelope
A smoothed version of a value that rises fast and falls slowly, so visuals hit on the beat and breathe after it.
Beat, tempo
The pulse you tap your foot to, and how fast it goes, in beats per minute (BPM).
Key, chord
The family of notes a song uses, and a few notes played together. Major usually sounds bright, minor darker.
Frame, FPS
One image on screen, and how many are shown per second. Smooth motion is 60 or more.
CPU, GPU
The computer's main processor, good at one thing after another; and the graphics card, good at the same thing on millions of pixels at once.
Shader
A tiny program that runs on the GPU and decides the color of each pixel.
Thread
One line of work inside a program. Juglar listens on one and draws on another, so neither waits for the other.
Rust
The programming language Juglar is written in. You don't need to read it to follow the documentation.
